How long do recruiters look at a resume? 6 things they see in 7.4 seconds

Ladders' eye-tracking study found recruiters spend an average of 7.4 seconds on a first resume scan. Where the eye goes in those seconds, which layouts win, and how to check your own CV.

A single job ad can draw dozens or even hundreds of applications. No recruiter reads every one from top to bottom. So how long do recruiters look at a resume? According to Ladders' eye-tracking study, the first scan takes 7.4 seconds on average [1][2]. This guide covers where the eye goes in those few seconds, which resume layouts win and how to check your own CV.

Where does 7.4 seconds come from?

In 2018 the job site Ladders published a study that recorded recruiters' eye movements as they read resumes. The average first scan took 7.4 seconds, up from about 6 seconds in the company's 2012 study [1][2]. Ladders partly attributed the shorter earlier figure to the recession, when applications surged [2].

Two caveats:

  • The data is not new. The study is from 2018. As AI screening tools spread, first-read habits may be changing.
  • The scope is limited. Critics note that the report does not say how many recruiters took part or which roles the resumes were for [3].

So 7.4 seconds is not a law, but it is a useful warning: if your resume is not readable at first glance, it may not get a second one.

Where does the eye go?

According to Ladders, resumes that performed well shared these traits [1][2]:

Winning layout Losing layout
Clear section headers and job titles Long sentences, blocks of paragraphs
One readable font Multiple columns, cramped page
Bold job title with bulleted achievements below Little white space, everything the same weight
Placement that follows F- and E-pattern reading No clear "path" for the eye

One more finding: when a recruiter is interested, they spend as much time on the second page as on the first [2]. A single page is not mandatory; the first page decides whether the second gets read.

6 things recruiters must see in 7.4 seconds

The list below is our practical suggestion based on the study's findings:

  1. Your name and target title. At the top, close to the title in the job ad.
  2. Your current or most recent title and company. The eye goes here first, so make it bold.
  3. Dates. Since when, and for how long? Right-aligned and consistent.
  4. One or two previous roles. Titles that show the direction of your career.
  5. Measurable achievements. Two to four bullets under each role, with numbers where possible: "Cut delivery time by 20%."
  6. Education. Short and lower on the page; move it up if you have little experience.

If you use AI to write your resume

Many candidates now use AI before they apply, and recruiters complain about resumes that look "too perfect" and alike. We covered the recruiter side in our guide on how to spot AI-generated resumes. For candidates the advice is simple: read what the AI wrote and put back the details and numbers that are yours.

Checklist: Does your resume pass the 7.4-second test?

  • Are your name and target title on the first line?
  • Are your latest title and company bold and immediately visible?
  • Are dates aligned and in one format?
  • Does each role have at least one achievement with a number?
  • Did you use one column, one font and enough white space?
  • Did you use bullets instead of paragraphs?
  • If you show the resume to a friend for 7 seconds and ask "what do I do?", do they get it right?

Frequently asked questions

How long do recruiters look at a resume?

According to Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study, the first scan takes 7.4 seconds on average; in its 2012 study it was about 6 seconds [1][2].

Should a resume be one page?

Not necessarily. The same study found that an interested recruiter spends as much time on the second page as on the first [2]. What matters is that the first page reads fast.

Which resume layout works best?

Clear headers, one readable font, bold job titles and bulleted achievements. Multi-column, cramped and wordy layouts performed poorly in the study [1][2].
