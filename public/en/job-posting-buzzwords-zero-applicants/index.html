Job posting buzzwords that cost you applicants: lessons from 1.37 million job ads

JobLeads analyzed 1.37 million US job postings and found that buzzwords like "motivated" and "hardworking" show up 4 to 10 times more often in ads that get clicks but zero applications. A guide with a word table, a Turkey legal note and a checklist.

Your job ad is getting clicks, but nobody applies. The problem may not be the pay or the role, but the words. A study published on October 21, 2026 by career platform JobLeads shows that ads written in vague language are far more likely to get zero applications [1][2]. This guide looks at which job posting buzzwords drive candidates away, what to write instead, and a checklist HR teams can apply today.

What did the study measure?

JobLeads analyzed 1,368,871 US job postings that drew at least one click between April and June 2026, across 11 industries (healthcare was excluded) [1]. Of these, 2,984 postings drew 10 or more clicks but not a single application [1]. The team compared these "zero-apply" postings with the baseline.

The first finding is counterintuitive: zero-apply postings list almost exactly as many skills as other postings (4.4 vs. 4.5), and median salaries differ by only 2-4% [1]. So it is not a wish list of requirements or low pay that drives readers away. As HR Dive put it, the difference is in how the posting says things, not what it asks for [2].

Job posting buzzwords to remove first

According to JobLeads, some words are heavily over-represented in zero-apply postings [1]:

Phrase in the ad How much more often in zero-apply ads Why it hurts What to write instead
"Motivated" 4.5x Unmeasurable, appears everywhere "Will run process X independently within 90 days"
"Hardworking" 4.3x Can read as a signal of long hours The actual schedule, shifts or hybrid days
"Following directions" 10x Suggests no autonomy Who the role reports to and which decisions it owns
"Laptop" / "smartphone" 3.7x / 2.2x Describes equipment, not the job; may imply the hire pays for it The named systems (ERP, HRIS, WMS) and equipment the company provides

Source: JobLeads study [1]; the "write instead" column is our own, built on the report's recommendation.

Quotable stat: "Motivated" appears in roughly 1 in 100 zero-apply postings, compared with about 1 in 450 across all clicked jobs, according to JobLeads [1].

The report's advice is direct: name the skills the role actually uses, the systems a new hire will work in, and what success looks like in the first few months [1].

HR's own postings struggle the most

One finding should sting for HR teams: HR postings make up 4.6% of clicked jobs but 5.9% of zero-apply jobs, 28% more than expected. Finance postings are 25% less likely than expected to land in the zero-apply group [1]. The function that writes most job ads struggles most with its own.

JobLeads suggests a reason: HR and consulting roles are often hired on subjective criteria like culture fit and described in the soft, trait-led language that over-indexes across the dataset [1].

The problem is widespread but has a tail. Of 2,393 companies with at least one zero-apply posting, 89% had only one. Yet the 35 companies with five or more account for one in ten of all zero-apply postings [1]. That pattern points to templates: the same vague paragraph copied into every ad.

The candidate side: little patience, many applications

HR Dive connects the findings to candidate behavior: Indeed data shows candidates may apply to as many as 90 roles, and an Employ Inc. survey found 71% of candidates expect an application to take less than 30 minutes, while 35% abandon applications [2]. In that market, a posting that does not explain the job simply gets skipped.

A separate JobLeads study from July 2026 scanned 55 million US job ads for red-flag phrases and found that cliché-heavy listings pay less [3]. Read together, the message is the same: candidates read your ad as a preview of the workplace.

Limits worth noting: the data covers clicks and applications on JobLeads only, applying on the platform sits behind a paid membership, and the findings are correlational, not causal [1].

A note for Turkey: the ad is also a legal document

The data is from US postings and should not be generalized to Turkey one-to-one. But employers in Turkey have an extra reason to check their wording: Article 6 of Law No. 6701 on the Human Rights and Equality Institution of Turkey states explicitly that the ban on discrimination against job applicants covers job advertisements [4]. Phrases like "young and dynamic" or "male candidates preferred" both drive applicants away and create legal risk.

Before and after: rewriting job ad wording

Before (vague) After (specific)
"We are looking for a motivated, hardworking team player in HR." "We are hiring an HR specialist to run payroll and personnel files for our 250-person plant, using SAP SuccessFactors."
"Detail-oriented and good at following directions." "Reports to the HR manager; approves leave and overtime requests."
"Fast-paced, dynamic environment." "Hybrid: three days on site, two days remote."
"Successful candidates will be contacted." "In the first 3 months: close payroll independently and cut onboarding from 5 days to 3."

Examples are ours, built on JobLeads' advice to name systems, skills and early success [1].

Job posting wording checklist (10 points)

  • Say what the role does and where in the first sentence
  • Delete unmeasurable adjectives: "motivated", "hardworking", "dynamic", "follows directions" [1]
  • List the systems and tools by name (ERP, HRIS, WMS) [1]
  • Write 2-3 concrete success criteria for the first 90 days [1]
  • Split requirements into "must have" and "nice to have"
  • State the work pattern (shifts, hybrid days) clearly
  • Remove discriminatory phrasing about age, gender or marital status [4]
  • Time your application process and keep it under 30 minutes [2]
  • Review zero-application postings monthly and look for shared patterns [1]
  • Update templates posting by posting, comparing results

Frequently asked questions

What are job posting buzzwords?

Vague, unmeasurable words such as "motivated", "hardworking" or "following directions". JobLeads found them 4.3 to 10 times more often in postings that got clicks but no applications [1].

Why does a job posting get clicks but no applications?

According to JobLeads, zero-apply postings ask for the same number of skills and pay about the same as others; vague wording is what sets them apart [1].

What should a job posting include instead?

The skills the role actually uses, the systems the new hire will work in, and what success looks like in the first few months [1].

Why do HR job postings struggle?

HR postings are 28% over-represented among zero-apply jobs. JobLeads suggests they are often described with subjective, trait-led language [1].

Is discriminatory wording in job ads illegal in Turkey?

Yes. Article 6 of Law No. 6701 extends the ban on discrimination to job advertisements [4].
