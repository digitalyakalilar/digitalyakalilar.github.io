AI saves employees time. But where does that time go? Research Gartner shared at its HR Symposium on October 26 gives an uncomfortable answer: 77% of board members expect AI to deliver productivity and cost savings, yet just 3% of organizations exceed their cost savings goals through productivity [1]. This post unpacks the gap and offers a practical framework for **how to measure AI productivity gains** at the workflow level.

## Expectation vs. reality in six numbers

| Finding | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Board members expecting AI productivity and cost savings | 77% | [1] |
| Organizations exceeding cost savings goals via productivity | 3% | [1] |
| CHROs reporting no leadership alignment on where AI creates value | 78% | [1] |
| Companies over budget on AI | 38% | [1] |
| Companies planning to spend more on AI next year | 85% | [1] |
| Share of AI-freed time redirected to more innovative work | 21% | [1] |

The figures come from a July 2026 Gartner survey of 297 CHROs and a 2Q26 survey of 1,865 employees [1]. The Register covered the findings under the headline "A whopping 3% of boardroom execs spy productivity and costs savings from AI" [3].

Quotable stat: **Only 21% of the time AI frees up for employees is redirected into more innovative work, according to a Gartner survey of 1,865 employees** [1].

## Why it isn't working: saved time is not productivity

In the words of Gartner's CV Viverito, there isn't a struggle to create capacity; it is already happening as organizations redesign work. The struggle is making that capacity usable and redirecting it to what is valuable [1]. Viverito adds that organizations will not generate cost savings from AI at this point; the greater opportunity is doing things that weren't possible before [1]. Put simply, saving time and gaining productivity are not the same: unmanaged hours never reach the cost line.

Gartner's Day 1 highlights show where the difference is made [2]:

| Approach | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Redesigning work to change its cost | More than twice as likely to exceed cost goals | [2] |
| Redirecting AI gains toward innovation | Nearly 4x more likely to outperform revenue goals | [2] |
| CHROs saying workflows and roles must change | 78% | [2] |
| Jobs requiring redesign every year through 2031 | 32 million | [2] |
| 2025 skills no longer relevant by 2030 | 34% | [2] |

Gartner also says AI transformation is moving four times faster than previous major technology shifts, that the number of skills required for the average job grows 4.8% a year, and that by 2028, 40% of employees will first be trained and coached by AI when entering a new role [2].

## Gartner's three actions

Gartner recommends three steps for CHROs [1]:

1. **Concentrate on value:** align leaders on the top bets for AI value instead of spreading investment thinly.
2. **Build for speed:** give business leaders self-serve tools to redesign work without waiting for HR at every step, and scale the AI expertise that already exists in the organization.
3. **Treat capacity gained as an asset:** do not leave freed time untracked; decide where it goes, and merge roles where work naturally belongs together.

Gartner names three skillsets behind success with AI: AI fluency, human connection and "human advantage" [1].

## Context: how big companies make capacity decisions

A concrete example of a capacity decision arrived this week. As Turkish tech outlet Webrazzi reported, citing Bloomberg, Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to nearly 60,000 of its roughly 223,000 employees while expecting to spend about $175 billion on AI infrastructure and data centers in 2026 [4]. Webrazzi notes the program is voluntary and that in a similar program earlier this year 30% of eligible employees accepted [4]. Microsoft did not tie the move directly to AI productivity, but the example shows why Gartner's warning matters: if freed capacity is not managed deliberately, the decision gets made elsewhere, for other reasons.

## How to measure AI productivity gains: a checklist

- [ ] Pick 3-5 workflows where AI should create value, together with leadership [1]
- [ ] Record a pre-AI baseline for each workflow (cycle time, errors, cost)
- [ ] Measure saved time from workflow data, not from employee self-reports alone
- [ ] Track monthly where freed time goes: innovative work, routine work, or lost [1]
- [ ] Give managers self-serve templates to redesign their team's work [1]
- [ ] Redesign at the role level, not just the task level; merge roles where it makes sense [1]
- [ ] Track AI budget and usage costs monthly [1]
- [ ] Plan training for AI fluency and human skills [1][2]
- [ ] Tie results to cost and revenue goals [2]
- [ ] Tell employees clearly how freed time will be used

## Frequently asked questions

### Does AI really increase productivity?

It saves time on individual tasks, but according to Gartner just 3% of organizations exceed their cost savings goals via productivity [1]. The difference lies in redesigning work.

### Where does AI-freed time go?

Gartner's survey of 1,865 employees found only 21% of freed time is redirected into more innovative work [1].

### How do you measure AI productivity gains?

At the workflow level rather than the task level, against a pre-AI baseline, while tracking where freed time is redirected. Gartner says organizations that redesign work are more than twice as likely to exceed cost goals [2].

### What do CHROs think about AI value?

In Gartner's survey of 297 CHROs, 78% said their leadership has no alignment on where AI creates value [1].

### Is Microsoft's buyout program about AI?

The company did not draw that link directly. Webrazzi reports it as part of efforts to control costs while AI investment rises [4].
